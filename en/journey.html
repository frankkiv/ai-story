Opening

The big picture: your data visits three places

At the top is your computer, in the middle are Anthropic's remote servers, and at the bottom is your phone. As you scroll, a line of light leads you into each one. The striped stretch in between is the internet: from your home, across the ocean, to the data center.

Chapter 1

Assembled on your computer

Step 1

You press Enter, and a program on your computer takes over

Where you type is Claude Code, a program installed on your computer. It is called the client: the side that talks to the server. At this point nothing has left your computer yet.

Step 2

First it reads files on your computer

The client reads the memory file CLAUDE.md (your project rules and preferences), the list of skills, the descriptions of MCP tools, and the full history of this conversation so far. All of these files live on your disk.

In plain wordsBefore you go see a consultant, you put your notebook, the company rules and all your past letters into your bag.

Step 3

Packed into one package, in a fixed order

The order is tool descriptions, system rules (with the skills list), memory, conversation history, and only then the line you just typed. The whole package is written as one JSON document, ready for the API's /v1/messages. Things that rarely change go first; the cache in the next chapter relies on that order.

Chapter 2

All the way to the server

Step 4

Locked before it leaves

The package goes out over HTTPS, an encrypted connection: the network equipment along the way can't read it. Your computer only connects outward and never opens a door for anyone to connect in.

In plain wordsThe letter goes into a locked box before the courier takes it. The courier knows where it is going, not what it says.

Step 5

Out of your home and across the ocean

The locked package leaves through your home Wi-Fi router, travels over your internet provider's network and an undersea cable, and arrives at a cloud data center that Anthropic uses. From Taiwan to a data center on the US West Coast, that is about 10,000 km one way, and a round trip takes roughly 0.1 to 0.2 seconds.

From this step on, the data is no longer on your computer. The checks, the computing and the writing of the answer all happen remotely.

In plain wordsLike an international courier: it leaves your doorstep and crosses the ocean to a building in another country. The internet is just much faster; the round trip takes a blink.

Step 6

The front desk checks who you are

The API (the agreed intake window between programs) checks your account or key and your usage quota, then hands the package to a GPU machine that is free.

Step 7

The text is split into tokens, a string of numbers

The model doesn't read letters. It reads tokens, the smallest units it handles: a word, part of a word, or a piece of punctuation. Each token has an ID number. The split and the numbers in the picture are only illustrations.

In plain wordsA sentence is broken into building blocks, and each block has a number on the back.

Step 8

Whatever matches last time is reused

The server compares this package with the last one, from the start, character by character. For the identical prefix it reuses the stored computation (the prompt cache); only the new part is computed now. By default the cache lives about 5 minutes; leave it too long and the whole package is computed again.

In plain wordsA regular who orders again within five minutes doesn't make the kitchen prep from scratch. What it remembers is the prepped ingredients; the chef's skill hasn't changed.

Step 9

The model writes one token at a time

On the GPUs, the model reads the whole package, works out the most likely next token, appends it, and works out the next one. This is called inference. The model's weights (everything it learned) do not change at all during this.

Chapter 3

How the answer comes back

Step 10

Sent back while it is being written

The answer is streamed: each small piece is sent as soon as it is computed, back along the same encrypted connection to your computer. That is why the words appear a bit at a time instead of all at once.

Step 11

If the model wants a file, your computer reads it

If the model needs to see the code first, it doesn't send back an answer. It sends a request, "use the Read tool on app.js" (tool_use). The client on your computer is what actually reads the file, and it asks you first before anything risky.

In plain wordsThe consultant doesn't come to your home. They write a note asking your assistant to fetch the document, and the assistant checks with you before anything important.

Step 12

The result is added, and the package goes through Chapter 2 again

What was read is added to the end of the package, which is encrypted, sent, checked, matched against the cache, and processed again. Everything before it matches the previous round, so almost all of it hits the cache. Going back and forth until the model stops asking for tools is the agent loop.

Step 13

Done, and the whole exchange is saved on your computer

This round's question, tool calls, results and answer are written to the conversation history on your computer (a .jsonl file in ~/.claude/projects/). Next time you ask, the client reads all of it again and resends it.

In plain wordsAI "remembers" what you talked about because your computer kept the record and attaches it again next time.

Chapter 4

How your phone stays in sync

Step 14

Computer and phone both connect to the same relay

With Remote Control on, your computer connects out to Anthropic's relay service, and the Claude app on your phone connects to it too. The relay only passes messages between the two, and keeps a copy of the conversation during the session so your phone can show it. It doesn't run your programs and can't touch your files.

In plain wordsThe phone is the remote, the computer is the TV, and a relay station in between passes the signal along.

Step 15

What you type on the phone reaches the computer through the relay

You type "Please fix it too" on your phone. The message goes to the relay first, then on to your computer, which adds it to the end of the package and starts again from Chapter 1. The Allow button on your phone sends your decision back to the computer the same way. So the computer has to stay on, and your files never leave home.

So

One message, four legs of a journey

Assembled on your computer, sent to a remote model, streamed back, and synced to your phone through a relay. The only thing that grows along the way is the record. The model itself doesn't change by a single bit.

To play with each layer yourself, try How it works · the context simulator. The three ways of syncing are compared in phone and computer sync.

Claude Code is used as the example. The token split and IDs are illustrative. The distance and delay from Taiwan to the US West Coast are estimates; real routes and data center locations vary. Check the official docs for prompt cache lifetimes. Fuller technical detail is in How it works.
