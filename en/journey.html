PART 2 walkthrough · Follow one message

After you press Enter,
where do your words go?

It starts with you at your computer, asking Claude Code "What is AI, really?". That question is packed up on your computer and sent across the ocean to a model in a remote data center. The model reads the whole package, works through it layer by layer, guesses the answer one word at a time, and decides whether it needs to search the web. Then the answer comes back, and your phone can see the same conversation. Four chapters, seventeen steps. Scroll down and look at one place at a time. Asking ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude in a phone app follows the same flow; the only difference is that the history and memory live on the provider's servers instead of your computer.

Opening

You sit at your computer and open Claude Code

Everyone keeps talking about AI, and you wonder what it actually is. So in Claude Code you type "What is AI, really?". At every step, "Your screen" at the top of the stage tells you what you see at that moment, and the map below shows what is really happening behind it.

Chapter 1

You ask a question at your computer

Step 1

You press Enter, and a program on your computer takes over

Where you type is Claude Code, a program installed on your computer. It is called the client: the side that talks to the server. At this point nothing has left your computer yet.

Step 2

First it reads files on your computer

The client reads the memory file CLAUDE.md (your project rules and preferences), the list of skills, the descriptions of MCP tools, and the full history of this conversation so far. All of these files live on your disk.

In plain wordsBefore you go see a consultant, you put your notebook, the company rules and all your past letters into your bag.

Step 3

Packed into one package, in a fixed order

The order is tool descriptions (web search is one of them), system rules (with the skills list and today's date), memory, conversation history, and only then the line you just typed. The whole package is written as one JSON document, ready for the API's /v1/messages. Things that rarely change go first; the cache in the next chapter relies on that order.

Chapter 2

The second or two you wait

Step 4

Locked before it leaves

The package goes out over HTTPS, an encrypted connection: the network equipment along the way can't read it. Your computer only connects outward and never opens a door for anyone to connect in.

In plain wordsThe letter goes into a locked box before the courier takes it. The courier knows where it is going, not what it says.

Step 5

Out of your home and across the ocean

The locked package leaves through your home Wi-Fi router, travels over your internet provider's network and an undersea cable, and arrives at a cloud data center that Anthropic uses. From Taiwan to a data center on the US West Coast, that is about 10,000 km one way, and a round trip takes roughly 0.1 to 0.2 seconds.

From this step on, the data is no longer on your computer. The checks, the computing and the writing of the answer all happen remotely.

In plain wordsLike an international courier: it leaves your doorstep and crosses the ocean to a building in another country. The internet is just much faster; the round trip takes a blink.

Step 6

The front desk checks who you are

The API (the agreed intake window between programs) checks your account or key and your usage quota, then hands the package to a GPU machine that is free.

Step 7

The text is split into tokens, a string of numbers

The model doesn't read letters. It reads tokens, the smallest units it handles: a word, part of a word, or a piece of punctuation. Each token has an ID number. The split and the numbers in the picture are only illustrations.

In plain wordsA sentence is broken into building blocks, and each block has a number on the back.

Step 8

Whatever matches last time is reused

The server compares this package with the last one, from the start, character by character. For the identical prefix it reuses the stored computation (the prompt cache); only the new part is computed now. By default the cache lives about 5 minutes; leave it too long and the whole package is computed again.

In plain wordsA regular who orders again within five minutes doesn't make the kitchen prep from scratch. What it remembers is the prepped ingredients; the chef's skill hasn't changed.

Step 9

The model reads the whole package first

The package goes into the model running on GPUs (chips that are good at doing huge numbers of calculations at once). The model first reads every token in one pass; this is called prefill. Each token it reads leaves behind an intermediate result called the KV cache. Every time a new word is written, the model looks back at these notes instead of rereading everything. The prompt cache in the last step stores exactly this kind of note.

In plain wordsBefore an exam you read the questions and the reference material once and jot the key points on a card; while answering you just check the card.

Step 10

One model, worked through layer by layer

The answer is not pieced together by several models. It is one model with dozens of identical layers stacked inside it (most companies don't publish the exact number; the picture is illustrative).

Each layer looks back at every token before it, works out which ones matter most right now (this is called attention), and passes its understanding up to the next layer. Higher layers are generally thought to handle more abstract things, like "this asks for a definition". The labels on each layer in the picture are only to help you imagine it; you can't actually read a layer like that.

In plain wordsLike a memo signed off up a chain of managers: each one adds a bit of judgment, and the last one makes the call.

Step 11

The top layer guesses the next word

The top layer gives every possible token a score and turns the scores into odds. After "AI is", "a" might be the most likely, then "the" (the numbers are illustrative). The model picks one, appends it, and uses the whole string to guess the next one. That is how the answer grows, one word at a time. This is called inference.

During all of this, the model's weights (everything it learned in training) do not change by a single number.

In plain wordsLike a word chain game: you add one word, read the whole line again, and think of the next.

Chapter 3

Search or not, then the answer

Step 12

Search the web? The model decides

The model doesn't go online for every question. Web search is one of the tools described at the start of the package, along with any MCP connectors you've added, and the model decides whether to use one, and which. "What is AI, really?" is a stable idea that it read plenty about in training, so it just answers.

If you asked "What's today's AI news?", what it learned stops at a certain date, so it would choose to call the search tool. How models keep up with new things in general is covered in How it works · Where new facts come from.

In plain wordsAsk a teacher what photosynthesis is and they just explain. Ask about today's headlines and they reach for the newspaper.

Step 13

If it searches, the results join the package

This question doesn't need it, but it is worth seeing once. Say you asked about today's news: the model first outputs a "search for this" request (tool_use), Anthropic's servers run the search, add page excerpts and links to the end of the package, and the model reads it again (steps 9 to 11) before writing an answer that cites its sources.

Tools like reading files or running commands are different: they go back to your computer to run, and the result is added to the package and sent again. Going back and forth until the model stops asking for tools is the agent loop.

Step 14

Sent back while it is being written

The answer is streamed: each small piece is sent as soon as it is computed, back along the same encrypted connection to your computer. That is why the words appear a bit at a time instead of all at once.

Step 15

Done, and the whole exchange is saved on your computer

This round's question and answer (plus any search results) are written to the conversation history on your computer (a .jsonl file in ~/.claude/projects/). Next time you ask, the client reads all of it again and resends it.

In plain wordsAI "remembers" what you talked about because your computer kept the record and attaches it again next time.

Chapter 4

You pick up your phone

Step 16

You leave your computer and see the same chat on your phone

You head out, open the Claude app on your phone, and see the same conversation. That is because Remote Control is on: your computer connects out to Anthropic's relay service, and the Claude app on your phone connects to it too. The relay only passes messages between the two, and keeps a copy of the conversation during the session so your phone can show it. It doesn't run your programs and can't touch your files.

In plain wordsThe phone is the remote, the computer is the TV, and a relay station in between passes the signal along.

Step 17

What you type on the phone reaches the computer through the relay

On your phone you ask a follow-up, "And today's AI news?". The message goes to the relay first, then on to your computer, which adds it to the end of the package and starts again from Chapter 1. This time the model chooses to search. If Claude Code asks for your OK first, the Allow button on your phone sends your decision back to the computer the same way. So the computer has to stay on, and your files never leave home.

So

Four things you saw, one map behind them

Assembled on your computer and sent to a remote model, which reads it all, works layer by layer and guesses one word at a time, searching only when it needs to. The answer streams back and syncs to your phone through a relay. The only thing that grows along the way is the record. The model itself doesn't change by a single bit.

To play with each layer yourself, try How it works · the context simulator. How models keep up with new things is in Where new facts come from. The three ways of syncing are compared in phone and computer sync.

Claude Code is used as the example. The token split and IDs, the number of layers and the word odds are illustrative. The distance and delay from Taiwan to the US West Coast are estimates; real routes and data center locations vary. Check the official docs for prompt cache lifetimes.
